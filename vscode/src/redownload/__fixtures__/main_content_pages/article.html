How zstd Compression Works & Why It Matters

Zstandard, usually shortened to zstd, is a lossless compression algorithm designed to give a strong compression ratio while staying fast enough to use on the hot path of a real system. It was created at Facebook and is now used across a huge range of software, from file systems to network protocols to database storage engines.

The core idea is to combine a modern entropy coder with a large search window. Where older algorithms had to trade ratio for speed in a fairly coarse way, zstd exposes a wide range of levels. At low levels it is comparable in speed to the fastest compressors while still beating them on ratio; at high levels it approaches the ratio of much slower algorithms while remaining decompression-fast.

Decompression speed is roughly constant regardless of the level used to compress, which is exactly what you want when data is written once and read many times. This is why a capture-first pipeline can afford to store the raw page compressed: the write cost is paid once and every later re-index reads it back quickly.

Dictionaries are another important feature. When you have many small, similar documents, training a dictionary lets the compressor reference shared structure that would otherwise be invisible within a single small payload. For web pages, which share a great deal of boilerplate markup, this can be a substantial win.

In practice the engineering trade-off is simple. Storing a few hundred kilobytes of compressed HTML per page is cheap, and it preserves the ability to re-extract content with better algorithms later. Lossy extraction at capture time, by contrast, throws away information that can never be recovered, because a page can never be captured again.
